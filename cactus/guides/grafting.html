Two plants, one developing connection

The scion is the plant above the union; the rootstock supplies the established root system. Healing involves adhesion and new vascular connections. A graft does not rewrite the scion’s seed parentage, and successful attachment does not certify either partner as pathogen-free.

Shimomura & Fuzihara · Vascular connection in cactus grafts (1977) ↗

Prepare and align

  1. Choose compatible, sound material. Work with actively growing, well-hydrated plants appropriate to the method. Quarantine unexplained symptoms instead of grafting them into a shared collection.
  2. Clean the workspace and tools. Remove plant debris, use an appropriate tool-disinfection procedure and let tools dry. A blade dipped in sulfur is not “sterilized.”
  3. Make fresh, even cuts. Bring the cut surfaces together promptly. Ordinary rooting cuttings callus first; graft surfaces must make fresh contact.
  4. Arrange vascular overlap. The two pieces do not need identical diameters. An offset position can help their vascular regions intersect; visual centering by itself is not the goal.
  5. Secure gently. Keep contact without crushing or slicing the scion with bands. Prevent sliding while the union develops.

What an alignment experiment established

In a 1977 study, young Notocactus submammulosus var. pampeanus scions were grafted onto Hylocereus trigonus. Vascular connections formed in nearly all aligned grafts by day 20, compared with 40% and 10% at 1 mm and 3 mm separation in that experiment.

This supports the importance of vascular proximity. Those percentages are not a success calculator for adult star cactus, Lophophora or every rootstock.

Shimomura & Fuzihara · Vascular connection in cactus grafts (1977) ↗

Choose a stock for a purpose

Pereskiopsis

Commonly used for small seedlings. Its leafy growth and handling differ from a large columnar stock; do not assume a fixed replacement deadline.

Pachanoi-type stock

A substantial columnar option. Keep the actual stock identity and source, not just “San Pedro.”

Myrtillocactus

Another columnar option. It is not uniquely both fast and permanent, and its performance depends on the pairing and conditions.

Selenicereus / Hylocereus

Keep both current and older literature names searchable. Tropical stock can change the cold requirements of an otherwise desert-type scion.

These are horticultural uses, not a ranked trial or universal compatibility chart.

Watch the union, not a countdown

Keep the union protected from direct sun, disturbance and standing moisture. Support the rooted stock appropriately; a blanket “never water any graft for a week” ignores differences between methods and plants. Monitor for pressure damage, slippage, shrivelling and rot.

Do not twist or pull at a fragile union to test it. Remove or adjust restraints before they damage tissue, taking account of attachment and growth. Healing time varies. New scion growth is more useful than a fixed seven-day promise.

Degrafting is a separate decision

A scion must be able to survive on its own roots before removal is sensible. Chlorophyll-free plants cannot simply be treated as normal green cuttings. Consider the remaining healthy tissue, rooting conditions and plant value; no fixed age makes every graft ready to remove.
